M.I.N.E.R.V.A.

An Educational Electoral Rumor Detection Simulator Using Symbolic Rule Engine via DE-GNN and QLattice Symbolic Regression for SHS Misinformation Literacy

The problem

Election season in the Philippines fills social feeds with rumors, and Senior High School students run into most of them before anyone teaches them how to check a post. Reading definitions of misinformation doesn’t build the habit of checking. Practicing on realistic examples might.

What we built

M.I.N.E.R.V.A. (Misinformation Investigation through Networked Embeddings for Rumor Verification and Awareness) is a Unity 6 Android game set in a fictional election season. Each in-game day, a feed of posts arrives in Chattr, the game’s made-up social app, and the player decides whether to Trust, Report or Ignore each one.

VERIdict then shows their credibility score and explains why every post was rated the way it was. VERIdex holds profiles of the fictional candidates, and V.E.R.I.T.A.S. gives players tools to check claims and trace where a post came from. Behind the game sits a pipeline that writes the content, scores it, and filters it so it’s safe for a classroom.

My part

I was the lead developer, project manager and team lead for our group of four. I built most of the generative-AI and explainability side of the pipeline, and I oversaw the pipeline as a whole, the testing, and the writing of the paper.

Two problems could have sunk the design. Graph models for rumor detection usually learn from who reposted or replied to whom, and our Filipino dataset had no repost or reply data at all. We kept the graph anyway: every post was linked to its ten nearest neighbors in embedding space, and the network trained on that similarity graph.

The second problem sat on my side of the pipeline. The generator kept putting real names into posts that were supposed to be fictional. Before anything reached a classroom, we replaced 1,061 person names across 397 cards and 370 place names across 207 cards, then ran a strict allowlist over what was left. Ten cards still failed it, so we cut them. The final pool went out with zero real people or places in it.

How it works

  1. Generate. A control-token-conditioned GPT-2 Tagalog model writes fictional election posts.
  2. Score. A dual-embedding graph neural network built on GraphSAGE feeds a Random Forest, and QLattice symbolic regression turns the credibility score into a formula you can actually read.
  3. Explain. A rule-based detector bank names the misinformation indicators in each post and turns them into cues a student can follow. Every cue traces back to a specific trigger in the text.
  4. Filter. Every card is pseudonymized and checked against an allowlist, so no real person or place ends up in classroom content.
  5. Play. The curated pool of 653 cards is drawn into eight 56-card decks for classroom play.

Alongside the pipeline we fine-tuned two transformer detectors, RoBERTa-Tagalog and multilingual DistilBERT, on the JCBlaise Filipino fake-news dataset and compared them across five random seeds.

What I learned

I learned to report the number we actually got. Our graph network reached 94.55% F1 on a single seed, close to the RoBERTa-Tagalog detector it was built on, so it reproduced that signal instead of beating it. That went into the paper as it happened. So did the fact that one of our twelve explanation cues, missing evidence, fired on 658 of 663 cards, which makes it close to useless for telling posts apart.

Most of the pressure was time. I carried the pipeline, the testing and the paper while working my student-assistant hours on top of a full course load. What kept the project standing was being clear about which results the thesis couldn’t do without: explanations that match their evidence, and no real names in front of students. Those held at 663 of 663 and zero leaks. Everything else was allowed to bend.

If I started over, I’d fix the evaluation first. Testing logistics and time pushed us into a pre-test and post-test taken by two separate groups of 50 students, so we can’t claim learning gains. Next time it would be the same students before and after, with a delayed check a few weeks later.

Error Propagation in Iterative Generative AI Text-to-SQL Refinement

Authors

Stack

Status
Submitted to IJACSA

The question

Say an AI writes a SQL query, the query fails, and all you tell it is “that failed.” If you let it try again, does it fix the query? We wanted to measure that instead of assuming it.

What we did

We took twelve tasks from the SQLite subset of Spider 2.0-Lite, spread over six databases, and gave them to four models through their free web apps: GPT-5.3, Claude Sonnet 4.6, DeepSeek V3.2 and Gemini 3 Flash. Each model got up to five tries per task, and the only feedback after a miss was “Result: FAIL.” That came to 48 runs and 240 logged turns.

Every turn was sorted into one of seven execution states:

  • Format error
  • Syntax error
  • Schema error
  • Runtime error
  • Timeout
  • Wrong result
  • Pass
Wrong result was where most turns landed. 155 of the 192 transitions went from wrong result straight back to wrong result. Pass was reached in 0 of 48 runs.

What we found

0 of 48Repair runs that ended in a correct query.
240240Turns logged across 12 tasks and 4 models.
155 / 192155 of 192Transitions that went from wrong result back to wrong result.

Most attempts got stuck writing queries that ran fine but returned the wrong answer. Some revisions made things worse: wrong results turned into runtime errors four times, timeouts four times and syntax errors twice. Under minimal feedback, retrying mostly repeated the mistake.

Why it matters

Telling a model that it failed wasn’t enough to get it unstuck. If you’re building a tool where a model checks and fixes its own output, test for this before you trust the retry loop. The follow-up the paper names is to run the same tasks with richer feedback, such as the engine’s error message or a diff of the expected output.

My part

I was the lead researcher and lead developer, and I’m the corresponding author. I wrote the Python and SQLite runner behind the study. It serializes each database schema, builds the prompt, pulls the SQL out of the model’s reply, executes it, labels the result with one of the seven states, writes the next turn’s feedback, and syncs every trace to the project workbook.

We had no budget for API access, so the study ran through the models’ free web apps. The runner built each prompt; we pasted it into the chat and handed the reply back. All 240 turns went through that loop by hand.

Then not one run passed. Zero out of 48 can look like an experiment that failed. We treated it as the finding instead, and because every turn was logged, we could show exactly how the models got stuck. That pattern became the paper.

The idea

Sungka is played on a board with two rows of seven houses and a store at each end, 98 shells in all. I wanted to see how far chess-engine search goes on it, and to make bot strength something a test can prove instead of something I judge by playing a few games.

What the first milestone includes

  • A rules engine and four bots: Random, Greedy, Minimax with alpha-beta pruning, a transposition table and move ordering, and Monte Carlo Tree Search.
  • An arena that plays paired games across worker threads. Each opening is played twice with the sides swapped, so the first-move advantage cancels out.
  • Glicko-2 ratings for every bot, with their uncertainty.
  • A strength gate built on the sequential probability ratio test (SPRT).
  • A web app, CI and deploy workflows, and the design written down as eight architecture decision records.

How the strength gate works

  1. Propose. A pull request changes the engine or its search.
  2. Play. The new engine plays the current one in paired games: same openings, sides swapped.
  3. Test. After each pair, SPRT checks whether there’s enough evidence to call the change stronger or weaker. It plays at least 30 pairs before it decides.
  4. Decide. A weaker engine fails the check instead of reaching the main branch.

So far the gate has run on my machine. Its first run on GitHub comes with the public repository.

Results

Measured on a 2-core Xeon with fixed node budgets, so the numbers don’t depend on how fast the machine is.

1887 ±521887, plus or minus 52Glicko-2 rating for Minimax at 50,000 nodes per move. It scored 80 of 80 against the Greedy bot.
1588 ±521588, plus or minus 52Rating for Monte Carlo Tree Search at 5,000 playouts per move.
+158plus 158Elo gained from alpha-beta pruning, with an interval of +101 to +223.
−58minus 58Elo of a deliberately weakened engine. The gate rejected it after 160 pairs, in 40 seconds.

What isn’t proven yet

Two smaller gains are still open. The transposition table measured +17 Elo and move ordering +26, but 40 pairs aren’t enough to call either one significant, so I list them as unproven until more games settle it.

Next

Milestone 2 adds an endgame tablebase, reuses the transposition table between moves, and brings a hint mode, a Fastify and WebSocket server, Postgres with game replays, and Docker.
